% ---- title -----------------------------------------------------------------
\title{\textbf{Making Double-Voting Expensive:\\ Deposits, Beacon-Drawn Groups and Key Transfers\\ for Anonymous Voting with Traceable Ring Signatures}\\[0.5em]
       \large Research Artifact, v0.5}
\author{Daniele Lin \and Niccol\`o Pagano}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Ring-signature voting systems usually rely on \emph{linkable} signatures:
a second ballot from the same key is recognised and discarded, but the
voter stays anonymous, so trying costs nothing. A \emph{traceable} ring
signature (Fujisaki--Suzuki, PKC~2007) reveals the public key of anyone
who signs two different messages under the same tag, while a single
signature stays anonymous. We describe and implement a voting design
that turns this property into an incentive on a permissionless
proof-of-work chain. Registering a voter locks a deposit, which the
chain burns if that key ever signs twice; voters sign inside fixed-size
groups drawn from a block-hash beacon after registration closes, so the
organiser cannot surround a target with its own keys; and in every round
but the last a voter may, instead of voting, transfer its right to a
fresh key that is regrouped, which widens its anonymity set. Signing
twice in a round, including voting and transferring, is a double-sign.
The design was sketched by the authors in 2021--22; we complete it,
state it as consensus rules of a reference chain, analyse what it does
and does not deter, and measure it. With groups of~8, a ballot is
548~bytes and verifies in about 2.7~ms regardless of electorate size,
against 64~kB and 0.32~s per ballot with one ring of 1\,000 voters. We
quantify the anonymity cost of sybils and the gain from transfers, and
report two implementation pitfalls found in the original prototype, one
of which silently destroyed anonymity.
\end{abstract}

% =========================================================================== %
\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================================== %

Ring signatures~\cite{RST01} let a signer prove membership in a set of
public keys without revealing which one it holds, which makes them a
natural ballot primitive. A plain ring signature, however, lets a voter
sign as often as it likes. Two families address this.
\emph{Linkable} ring signatures~\cite{LWW04,CLSAG2020} attach a tag
that is equal for two signatures by the same key, so duplicates can be
dropped. \emph{Traceable} ring signatures~\cite{FS07} go further: two
signatures by the same key on \emph{different} messages reveal the key
itself, and the scheme guarantees that an honest signer cannot be framed.

Linkability answers ``is this a duplicate?''. Traceability also answers
``who did it?'', and that answer can carry a consequence. On a chain
with a native currency, the consequence can be automatic: a deposit
that the protocol burns when the trace rule fires. Nobody needs to
adjudicate, because the evidence is two valid signatures that anyone can
check. The same ``anonymous if honest, slashable if not'' idea has been
proposed for proof-of-stake validators~\cite{CasperTRS}; to our
knowledge it has not been worked out for voters, where it interacts with
two further problems: ring signatures cost $O(n)$, so voters must be
split into groups, and whoever forms the groups controls each voter's
anonymity.

This artifact builds on a design that the authors sketched in 2021--22,
together with a prototype (kept in \texttt{legacy/}). Its documents and
code fix a \emph{group size} in the poll announcement, require voters to
sign their own registration (proof of possession), let a third party
pay for it, distinguish \emph{fees} (paid to miners) from
\emph{forfeits} (locked eVotes that nobody receives), and encode two
kinds of signed message: a vote, or a transfer ``to another group with
another key''. The prototype never connected these pieces, and its
signature implementation had two bugs, one of which made every
signature de-anonymisable (\cref{sec:legacy}). We complete the design,
fix the primitive, and make every rule part of block validation.

\subsection{Research question}
\label{sec:rq}

\emph{Can traceability, an identity revealed only on misbehaviour, be
turned into an enforceable deterrent for voting on a permissionless
chain, and what do the groups this requires cost in anonymity?}
Concretely: what does a deposit burned on tracing deter, and what not;
how can groups be formed so that no participant chooses them; can
voters recover anonymity lost to small groups by moving between them;
and what does all of this cost compared with one ring for everybody and
with a permissioned bulletin board.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The design} (\cref{sec:design}): per-voter deposits
      burned on tracing; groups of at least $k$ members ordered by a hash
      keyed with the block hash that precedes the freeze; ``vote or
      move'' rounds with key transfers; proof of possession at
      registration; a lifecycle driven only by block timestamps, with no
      transaction that closes a poll or publishes a tally.
\item \textbf{A reference implementation as consensus rules}
      (\texttt{chain/}, \texttt{voting/groups.py},
      \texttt{voting/tally.py}): a SHA-256 proof-of-work chain whose
      block validation enforces the prescribed difficulty, heaviest-chain
      fork choice, deposits and escrow, group formation, round
      discipline, per-group trace bookkeeping, tally and settlement.
\item \textbf{A clean FS07 implementation} over Ristretto255 with RFC~9380
      hash-to-curve (\texttt{trs/}), a message-aware \Trace{} that also
      handles groups of one, and a Schnorr proof of possession.
\item \textbf{Analysis} (\cref{sec:analysis}) of deterrence, framing,
      group-assignment bias, sybils, transfer anonymity and the
      leakage of per-group tallies.
\item \textbf{Evaluation} (\cref{sec:eval}): cost versus group size,
      end-to-end chain cost with one ring and with groups, and a Monte
      Carlo estimate of the anonymity left to honest voters under sybil
      registration, before and after transfers.
\item \textbf{Lessons from the prototype} (\cref{sec:legacy}): a hash
      into the group with a known discrete logarithm, and an exponent
      inverted modulo the wrong number. Both are easy to make and both
      pass a demo.
\item \textbf{A permissioned baseline} (Architecture~A, \cref{sec:baseline}):
      a threshold-signed bulletin board with a witness federation, one
      ring and no deposits, built on the same primitive and records.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Non-goals}

We do not provide receipt-freeness or coercion resistance, a
peer-to-peer network layer, a side-channel-hardened implementation, or
machine-checked proofs. The chain is a reference model in which the
rules of the design can be executed and tested, not a deployable
network.

\section{Preliminaries}
% =========================================================================== %

We work in a cyclic group $\G$ of prime order $q$ with generator $g$, in
which the Decisional Diffie--Hellman (DDH) problem is assumed hard. We
instantiate $\G$ as Ristretto255~\cite{Ristretto2020},
\[ q = 2^{252} + 27742317777372353535851937790883648493, \]
a prime-order group built on Curve25519 that removes the cofactor
pitfalls of raw Edwards25519.

The scheme needs three hash functions modelled as random oracles,
$\Hzero, \Hone : \{0,1\}^* \to \G$ and $\Htwo : \{0,1\}^* \to \Zq$.
$\Hzero$ and $\Hone$ are the RFC~9380~\cite{RFC9380} random-oracle
hash-to-curve for Ristretto255 (\texttt{XMD:SHA-512}); $\Htwo$ uses the
same expander followed by a wide reduction modulo $q$. Each oracle has its
own domain-separation tag, fixed in \texttt{trs/trs.py}:
\begin{lstlisting}[style=py]
DST_H0 = b"FS07-TRS-v1-RISTRETTO255-XMD:SHA-512-H0"
DST_H1 = b"FS07-TRS-v1-RISTRETTO255-XMD:SHA-512-H1"
DST_H2 = b"FS07-TRS-v1-RISTRETTO255-XMD:SHA-512-H2"
\end{lstlisting}

\begin{assumption}[DDH in $\G$]
For uniformly random $a, b, c \in \Zq$, the distributions
$(g^a, g^b, g^{ab})$ and $(g^a, g^b, g^c)$ are computationally indistinguishable.
\end{assumption}

It matters that $\Hzero$ is a genuine hash to the group: nobody may know
$\log_g \Hzero(\cdot)$. If $h = g^t$ with $t$ known, then
$h^{x_j} = \pk_j^{\,t}$ is computable for every member, and the trace
tag of a signature names its signer (\cref{sec:legacy}).

% =========================================================================== %
\section{The Fujisaki--Suzuki traceable ring signature}
\label{sec:construction}
% =========================================================================== %

A \emph{tag} is a pair $L = (I, R)$ of an \emph{issue} $I$ and a ring
$R = (\pk_1, \ldots, \pk_n)$; in our application the issue is the poll
identifier (suffixed with the round number after round~0) and the ring
is a group. Positions are $1$-indexed so that $i^{-1} \bmod q$ exists.

\paragraph{\KeyGen.} $x \sample \Zq^*$, $\pk = g^x$.

\paragraph{$\Sign(x_i, L, m)$.}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item $h \gets \Hzero(I \,\|\, R)$, $A_0 \gets \Hone(I \,\|\, R \,\|\, m)$.
\item $\sigma_i \gets h^{x_i}$: the signer's \emph{trace tag}, which depends
      on $(L, x_i)$ but not on $m$.
\item $A_1 \gets (\sigma_i / A_0)^{1/i}$, with $1/i$ computed modulo~$q$.
      Every position $j$ now has a column $\sigma_j \gets A_0 \cdot A_1^{\,j}$,
      and the column at $j = i$ equals the trace tag.
\item A Fiat--Shamir $\Sigma$-OR proof that for some $j$,
      $\log_g \pk_j = \log_h \sigma_j$: for $j \ne i$ pick $c_j, z_j$ at
      random and set $a_j = g^{z_j}\pk_j^{c_j}$, $b_j = h^{z_j}\sigma_j^{c_j}$;
      for $j = i$ set $a_i = g^w$, $b_i = h^w$; let
      $c^* = \Htwo(L, A_0, A_1, a_1..a_n, b_1..b_n)$,
      $c_i = c^* - \sum_{j \ne i} c_j$, $z_i = w - c_i x_i$.
\item Output $(A_1, c_1..c_n, z_1..z_n)$: $36 + 64n$ bytes in our encoding.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{\Verify} recomputes $h$, $A_0$, the columns, every $a_j, b_j$
and $c^*$, and accepts iff $\sum_j c_j = c^*$.

\paragraph{\Trace.} Given two valid signatures under the same tag on
$m_1, m_2$, compute both column vectors. The signer's column depends only
on $(L, x)$, so two signatures by the same key share it; for $j$ other
than the signer's position the columns depend on the message. FS07
outputs:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item $m_1 = m_2$ and all $n$ columns equal: \textbf{linked} (same signer,
      same message; typically a copy);
\item $m_1 \ne m_2$ and exactly one column equal: \textbf{the key at that
      position} (a double-sign);
\item otherwise: \textbf{independent}.
\end{itemize}
Our \texttt{trs.trace} decides on the messages first. Counting matching
columns alone breaks down for $n = 1$, where ``all columns'' and
``exactly one column'' coincide; groups of one do occur in transfer
rounds (\cref{sec:transfers}), so the distinction is needed.

FS07 prove the scheme unforgeable, anonymous (a single signature hides
its signer under DDH), tag-linkable, and \emph{exculpable}: an adversary
that does not know $x_j$ cannot produce two signatures traced to $\pk_j$.
Exculpability is what makes automatic punishment safe.

\paragraph{Proof of possession.} Registration carries a Schnorr proof
$(R = g^r,\ s = r + c x)$ with $c = H(R \,\|\, \pk \,\|\, \mathit{ctx})$
and context $\texttt{register-v1} \,\|\, \mathit{election\_id} \,\|\,
\mathit{handle}$ (\texttt{trs/pop.py}). It stops anyone from registering a
key it does not control.

% =========================================================================== %
\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsection{Participants and assets}

The chain has an account ledger of \emph{eVotes} keyed by Ed25519 public
keys. A \emph{creator} opens a poll; a \emph{sponsor} registers voters
and pays for them (it may be the voter, the creator or a third party);
\emph{voters} hold TRS keys; \emph{miners} produce blocks. Ballots and
key transfers are the only transactions without an account: they carry
no sender, no signature at the chain layer and no fee, so they do not
link a voter to an account.

\subsection{Poll parameters}

A \textsf{setup\_poll} transaction, signed by the creator, fixes the
options and
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item the schedule $\mathit{registration\_close} \le \mathit{voting\_open}
      < \mathit{voting\_close}$, with $\mathit{voting\_open}$ in the future;
\item the \emph{group size} $k$ ($0$ for one ring, otherwise $k \ge 2$);
\item the number of \emph{rounds} $r \ge 1$; the voting window
      $[\mathit{voting\_open}, \mathit{voting\_close})$ is split evenly
      among them;
\item the \emph{deposit} $D \ge 0$ locked per registered voter.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Registration}

A \textsf{register\_voter} transaction is signed by the sponsor and
carries the voter's TRS public key, a handle, and the proof of
possession. It is valid only before $\mathit{registration\_close}$, for a
key not yet registered in the poll, with a valid proof, and with the
deposit equal to the poll's $D$. The sponsor pays the registration fee
(to the miner) and $D$ (to the poll's escrow, which no account
controls).

\subsection{Groups drawn from a beacon}
\label{sec:groups}

Phase changes happen at the start of the first block whose timestamp
crosses a deadline. The first block with timestamp
$\ge \mathit{voting\_open}$ freezes the registered keys $P$ and splits
them into groups, using as beacon $\beta$ that block's
$\mathit{prev\_hash}$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item if $k = 0$ or $|P| < 2k$, one group $P$;
\item otherwise sort $P$ by $\mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}(\dst_G
      \,\|\, I_0 \,\|\, \beta \,\|\, \pk)$, with $\dst_G =
      \texttt{e-ring-voting-groups-v1}$ and $I_0$ the round-0 issue,, cut it into $g = \lfloor |P|/k
      \rfloor$ consecutive groups and give the $|P| \bmod g$ leftovers one
      each, so every group has between $k$ and $2k-1$ members;
\item each group is sorted by key bytes, which is its ring order.
\end{enumerate}
A poll with fewer than two registrations is aborted and its deposits
refunded. Every key was registered, and the creator's choices were
final, before $\beta$ existed, so neither the creator nor a sponsor can
place a key next to a target. The miner of the block that becomes
$\beta$ has some influence; we bound it in \cref{sec:bias}.

\subsection{Rounds: vote or move}
\label{sec:transfers}

In round $\rho$, a key in group $G$ spends its right once by signing,
under the tag $(I_\rho, G)$, either
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item a \textbf{ballot}, message $\texttt{ballot-v1} \,\|\, \mathit{choice}$, or
\item a \textbf{key transfer}, message $\texttt{key-transfer-v1} \,\|\,
      \pk'$, naming a fresh key $\pk'$ (allowed in every round but the
      last; $\pk'$ must not already belong to the poll).
\end{itemize}
Both kinds use the same tag, so a key that votes and moves in the same
round has signed two different messages and is traced. At the first
block past the end of round $\rho$, the accepted transfers' target keys
form the members of round $\rho + 1$, which are grouped again with the
same algorithm, the issue $I_{\rho+1}$ and a fresh beacon. Messages are accepted only in their
own round, and only if they verify against the group they name.

The observer sees which group a transfer was signed in, but not by
whom. A ballot cast by $\pk'$ in round $\rho+1$ therefore hides among
all the round-$\rho$ members of the origin groups of every key in its new
group, which is a superset of the voter's original group
(\cref{sec:anon-transfer}).

\subsection{Admission and classification}

Each group keeps a \emph{tag book} (\texttt{voting/tally.py}) that
indexes recorded messages by $(j, \sigma_j)$. When a ballot or transfer
arrives and verifies, the book looks up the earlier messages that share
any column with it:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item none: \textbf{fresh}, recorded;
\item one, different message: \textbf{evidence}, recorded; the signer is
      now exposed;
\item one with the same message: \textbf{replay}, invalid;
\item two already: \textbf{saturated}, invalid (nothing more to learn).
\end{itemize}
Every signer therefore has at most two messages on chain, and a copy of
someone else's ballot cannot be included at all. At the close, connected
components of messages sharing columns are signers; a component with one
distinct message is accepted, a component with two is a double-signer
whose messages are void, and the column position shared by different
messages names the culprit.

\subsection{Close and settlement}

At the first block with timestamp $\ge \mathit{voting\_close}$ the chain
computes the tally from the accepted ballots of every round, lists the
culprits per round, \emph{burns} the deposit of every round-0 culprit,
and refunds every other deposit to its sponsor. Burned eVotes go to
nobody: if they went to the miner, a miner would profit from evidence it
could manufacture with keys it had registered itself. No transaction
publishes or confirms the result; every node computes it while
replaying the chain, and the auditor is that replay
(\texttt{chain/auditor.py}).

Keys that arrived by transfer have no deposit behind them, since tying
them to one would undo the transfer's unlinkability. A double-sign by
such a key voids its messages but burns nothing (\cref{sec:limits}).

\subsection{Chain}

Blocks carry $(\mathit{prev\_hash}, \mathit{height}, \mathit{difficulty},
\mathit{timestamp}, \mathit{miner}, \mathit{tx\_root}, \mathit{nonce})$
and a transaction list; the proof of work is
$\mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}(\mathit{header})$ having $\mathit{difficulty}$
leading zero bits. As in the prototype, the difficulty moves by one bit
when the median interval of the last 11 blocks leaves
$[T/2, 3T/2]$ around $T = 60$\,s; unlike the prototype's code, the
required difficulty is a validation rule, so a block mined at any other
difficulty is invalid. Nodes follow the valid chain with the largest
$\sum 2^{\mathit{difficulty}}$. Block application is atomic, and the
coinbase must equal the block reward plus fees; deposits never reach it.

% =========================================================================== %
\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
% =========================================================================== %

The full threat model is in \texttt{paper/threat\_model.md}. We assume
DDH in Ristretto255, random oracles for the hashes, Ed25519
unforgeability, and an honest majority of hash power for chain
liveness and safety.

\subsection{What the deposit deters, and what it does not}

\textbf{Double-voting does not pay.} Two different messages by a key in a
round void both, so a double-sign never adds a vote. In particular the
attack that transfers make possible, voting in round $\rho$ and moving to
a key that votes again in round $\rho+1$, is a double-sign in round
$\rho$: the ballot is void and the transfer is dropped before regrouping.

\textbf{It costs.} Without a penalty, signing twice is free; with
linkable signatures the signer is not even identified. Here the key is
named on chain and, for a registered key, its sponsor loses $D$. This
gives the sponsor, which is often the organisation that vouched for the
voter, a direct reason to vet voters and protect their keys, and it gives
a voter a reason not to hand a copy of its key to someone else: if both
use it, both lose.

\textbf{It cannot frame.} By exculpability, only a party that knows $x_j$
can produce two messages traced to $\pk_j$. Replaying honest ballots does
not help: a copy is \emph{linked}, never a double-sign, and the tag book
refuses it. The rewrite that preceded this version got this wrong
(\cref{sec:legacy}).

\textbf{What it does not do.} The deposit prices \emph{misbehaviour}, not
\emph{registration}: a sybil key that behaves gets its deposit back, so
for an adversary that registers keys only to fill groups, the deposit is
locked capital, not a cost. Registration fees and the sponsor's
on-chain identity are what price sybils. Nor does the deposit stop a
voter who sells its vote and abstains, or a coercer who watches the voter
sign.

\subsection{Who chooses the groups}
\label{sec:bias}

Registrations close before $\beta$ exists, so the creator and sponsors
choose keys blind. The miner of the block that becomes $\beta$ can discard
a block whose hash gives an assignment it dislikes and mine another; it
can also choose its timestamp, and so whether its block is the one that
crosses $\mathit{voting\_open}$. Each discarded block costs that block's
reward and the expected work to replace it. If one draw isolates a given
honest voter (puts it in a group with no other honest member) with
probability $p$, an adversary needs about $1/p$ discarded blocks to
isolate that voter. With half of the keys controlled by the adversary
and $k = 8$, \cref{tab:anon} measures $p \approx 0.8\%$, so about $130$
discarded blocks per target; with $k = 4$ the same adversary
succeeds on $\approx 13\%$ of honest voters with no grinding at all.
A VDF or commit--reveal beacon would remove the bias (\cref{sec:future}).

\subsection{Sybils}

Sybil keys reduce the \emph{honest} part of a group: with sybil share $s$,
an honest voter in a group of $k$ expects about $1 + (k-1)(1-s)$ honest
members. They cannot be detected, because a sybil that never
double-signs behaves exactly like an honest voter, and the problem is the
same one anonymity systems with groups face~\cite{Buddies2013}. The
design can only make sybils costly (fees, accountable sponsors) and pick
$k$ large enough that the honest share stays useful; \cref{tab:anon}
gives the numbers.

\subsection{Anonymity after transfers}
\label{sec:anon-transfer}

Let $O(\pk')$ be the round-$\rho$ group in which the transfer to $\pk'$
was signed. The observer knows $O(\pk')$ but not which member signed it.
A ballot by $\pk'$ in round-$(\rho+1)$ group $G'$ could have been cast by
any round-$\rho$ member of $\bigcup_{\pk'' \in G'} O(\pk'')$, which
contains the voter's own original group. Under this observer model a
transfer therefore never shrinks the anonymity set, and widens it by up
to $|G'|$ origin groups; \cref{tab:anon} measures the effect. Counting
arguments (how many members of an origin group voted or moved) can
narrow the set further and are not captured by this metric, which is
an upper bound on what a counting observer is left with.

\subsection{What groups leak}

Ballots name their group, so the chain publishes a tally \emph{per
group}. If every member of a group votes the same way, everyone's vote
in that group is known; more generally the per-group distribution is
public. A single ring leaks the same information only at the scale of
the whole electorate. This is a real cost of groups and a second reason,
besides sybils, not to make $k$ small; transfers help, because the
round-$(\rho+1)$ groups mix voters from different origins.

\subsection{Censorship and front-running}

Anonymous transactions pay no fee, so a miner has no direct incentive to
include them and can exclude ballots at will; with an honest majority of
hash power they are eventually included. A transfer cannot be redirected,
because the target key is inside the signed message; front-running it
with a copy only includes the same transfer earlier. A ballot or
transfer submitted after its round ends is invalid, so late inclusion
by a censoring majority would disenfranchise; we do not address this.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Transferred keys cannot be slashed.} This is a trade-off
      between accountability and the unlinkability the transfer exists
      for.
\item \textbf{Beacon bias} as in \cref{sec:bias}.
\item \textbf{No coercion resistance or receipt-freeness}; a voter can
      prove its ballot by revealing $x$.
\item \textbf{Chain security is economic}~\cite{ParkSpecter2021}: an
      adversary that rents a majority of hash power for the voting window
      can censor and reorganise. The design does not change this.
\item \textbf{Implementation}: Python glue is not constant time
      (group operations are libsodium); the chain has no network layer.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
% =========================================================================== %

\begin{lstlisting}[style=shell]
trs/        group.py hash.py trs.py pop.py serialize.py _libsodium.py
voting/     records.py groups.py tally.py          # shared election logic
            log.py manager.py voter.py witness.py auditor.py cli.py  # baseline A
chain/      block.py transactions.py state.py mining.py node.py
            client.py auditor.py cli.py             # the design (B)
tests/      165 tests      bench/  bench_trs, bench_chain, bench_groups
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Primitive.} \texttt{trs.group} wraps libsodium's Ristretto255
through a direct cffi binding; point decoding always checks group
membership. \texttt{trs.hash} implements \texttt{expand\_message\_xmd}
(RFC~9380 \S5.3.1, including the oversize-DST path) and composes it with
\texttt{crypto\_core\_ristretto255\_from\_hash}. All secret randomness
comes from \texttt{os.urandom} (64 bytes reduced modulo $q$). Rings are
encoded with a 4-byte length prefix and 32-byte points; order is part
of the tag.

\paragraph{Consensus.} \texttt{chain/state.py} holds every rule of
\cref{sec:design}: phase changes at block timestamps
(\texttt{advance\_polls}, also used by clients through
\texttt{state\_for\_next\_block} to learn the groups a ballot in the next
block must use), escrow and settlement, and the per-group tag books.
\texttt{chain/client.py} builds registrations, ballots and transfers for a
voter. The CLI mines one block per command with an explicit timestamp, so
a complete poll with groups, a transfer and a double-sign runs in about a
second (README, ``Quickstart --- the chain'').

\paragraph{Tests.} The 165 tests cover the group wrapper, hash-to-curve,
the primitive (each ring position, negative cases, trace outcomes
including $n = 1$, proof of possession), property-based tests, group
assignment (sizes, determinism, beacon dependence, sybil frequency),
classification (including the replay-framing regression), the chain
(difficulty rule, fork choice, ledger, registration rules, groups,
rounds, transfers, vote-and-move, settlement), and the baseline.

\section{Baseline: a permissioned bulletin board}
\label{sec:baseline}
% =========================================================================== %

Architecture~A (\texttt{voting/}) answers the same election with the
standard alternative to a chain: a hash-chained append-only log whose
entries are co-signed by a $t$-of-$N$ cohort of named publishers, with a
$k$-of-$M$ witness federation that co-signs log heads so that a cohort
which shows different logs to different people produces public evidence
of equivocation (the Certificate Transparency pattern). The lifecycle is
explicit
($\textsf{Setup} \to \textsf{Registration}^* \to \textsf{Ring} \to
\textsf{Ballot}^* \to \textsf{Closed} \to \textsf{Tally}?$), all voters
share one ring, and there are no deposits, groups or transfers: a double
vote is traced and voided, but costs nothing. The cohort refuses linked
copies of a ballot, and the auditor recomputes the tally from the log.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Baseline A versus the design (B).}
\label{tab:compare}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{l X X}
\toprule
& \textbf{A: federated board} & \textbf{B: this design} \\
\midrule
Who orders entries   & $N$ named publishers ($t$ to sign)      & anyone with hash power \\
Equivocation         & witness checkpoints expose it            & fork choice; reorgs possible \\
Ring                 & one ring of all voters, $O(N)$ per ballot & groups of $k$..$2k{-}1$, $O(k)$ per ballot \\
Who forms rings      & the cohort publishes the ring            & a beacon after registration closes \\
Double vote          & traced and voided                        & traced, voided, deposit burned \\
Anonymity widening   & none                                     & key transfers between rounds \\
Tally                & claimed by the cohort, recomputed by auditors & computed by every node; nobody publishes it \\
Finality             & seconds                                  & probabilistic \\
Main residual risk   & a colluding cohort can censor            & hash-power rental~\cite{ParkSpecter2021}; beacon bias \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

A is the right choice when a small, accountable authority set exists and
fast finality matters. B is for settings in which nobody, including the
organiser, should host the record or form the rings, and in which
misbehaviour should cost money without an authority deciding it.

\Cref{tab:origin} lists the sources. One element is ours: in the 2022
procedure the forfeit is locked by the poll's manager; we attach a forfeit
to every registered voter and make tracing its trigger.

Reading the prototype back turned up two bugs in the signature code,
each verified by running it:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{$\Hzero$ had a known discrete logarithm.} The hash oracles
      mapped data to $g^{t}$ with $t$ derived from the data by a
      deterministic generator (\texttt{ring.py:111--120}). Anyone can
      compute $t$ for $h = \Hzero(I \,\|\, R)$ and then $\pk_j^{\,t} =
      h^{x_j}$ for every member, and compare with the columns of a
      signature: the signer's position follows from public data alone.
      Signatures verified and traced correctly, so nothing in a demo
      showed it.
\item \textbf{$1/i$ was computed modulo $p$} (the field) instead of $q$
      (the group order) (\texttt{ring.py:147}). Only the signer at
      position~1, where the inverse is $1$ either way, produced verifying
      signatures; the prototype's demo signed at position~1.
\end{itemize}
The node also adjusted difficulty in opposite directions when mining and
when validating, and awaited inside a constructor; neither layer was
connected to votes.

A 2026 rewrite that preceded this version replaced the chain with the
bulletin board of \cref{sec:baseline}, used one ring, and dropped
deposits, groups and transfers; it also attributed the scheme to
Scafuro and Zhang~\cite{ScafuroZhang2021}, whose one-time traceable ring
signature is a different, hash-based construction. Its auditors
attributed a pair of identical copies of a ballot to the key at ring
position~1, so anyone could replay two honest ballots, void both votes and
blame an innocent voter; and its chain accepted blocks at any
difficulty. Both are fixed here, with regression tests.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
% =========================================================================== %

\textbf{Ring signatures for voting.} Chirotonia~\cite{Chirotonia2021}
runs elections on Ethereum with linkable ring signatures: one ring per
election, duplicates discarded, no penalty. This is the common pattern
for ring-signature voting on blockchains; FS07 already list voting
among the applications of traceability.

\textbf{Slashable anonymity.} A proposal for privacy-preserving Casper
FFG~\cite{CasperTRS} uses traceable ring signatures so that validators
stay anonymous unless they equivocate, in which case they are identified
and slashed. Our deposits apply the same principle to voters; groups,
beacon assignment and transfers have no counterpart there.

\textbf{Ring-signature variants.} Linkable schemes reach logarithmic size
(Triptych~\cite{Triptych2020}, Lelantus~\cite{Jivanyan2019}, one-out-of-many
proofs~\cite{GK15}); traceable schemes with sub-linear size also
exist~\cite{Fujisaki2011}. Scafuro and Zhang~\cite{ScafuroZhang2021}
give a one-time traceable ring signature from hash functions only,
which would make the design post-quantum at a cost in size. Threshold
and ad-hoc-group ring signatures~\cite{BSS02} and $k$-linkable
signatures~\cite{KLinkable2025} relax ``one use'' in other directions.

\textbf{Groups and intersection attacks.} Splitting users into
anonymity groups and the effect of sybils and of repeated observations
on them are studied in Buddies~\cite{Buddies2013}; long-term anonymity of
ring signatures across many uses is discussed in~\cite{LongTerm2023}.
Our transfers are the reverse operation: a voter leaves a group on
purpose, once, to enlarge its set.

\textbf{Blockchain voting.} Park et al.~\cite{ParkSpecter2021} argue
that blockchains add attack surface to Internet voting; our design does
not answer their objections about client security and coercion, and
inherits the economic security of proof of work.

To our knowledge, no published system combines a traceable ring
signature with a deposit burned on tracing, beacon-assigned voter groups
and anonymity-widening transfers.

\section{Open problems}
\label{sec:future}
% =========================================================================== %

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Slashing transferred keys} without linking them to their
      origin, e.g.\ by moving a deposit along with the transfer through
      a blind or zero-knowledge commitment.
\item \textbf{An unbiasable beacon}: a VDF over $\mathit{prev\_hash}$ or a
      commit--reveal among sponsors.
\item \textbf{Pricing}: the deposit and registration fee needed for a
      given $k$ and adversary budget, and incentives for miners to include
      fee-less ballots (e.g.\ paid from the poll's setup fee).
\item \textbf{Per-group tally leakage}: tallying across groups without
      publishing per-group counts.
\item \textbf{Logarithmic-size traceable signatures}~\cite{Fujisaki2011},
      which would allow larger $k$ at the same cost.
\item \textbf{Formal models}: ProVerif/Tamarin for the protocol and
      EasyCrypt for the primitive.
\item \textbf{Coercion resistance}~\cite{JCJ2005,Civitas2008}.
\end{enumerate}
