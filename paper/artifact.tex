\title{\textbf{One Key, One Use, or Lose the Deposit:\\ Anonymous Voting on a Proof-of-Work Chain\\ with One-time Traceable Ring Signatures}\\[0.5em]
       \large Research Artifact, v1.0}
\author{Daniele Lin \and Niccol\`o Pagano}
\date{November 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Ring-signature voting systems usually rely on \emph{linkable} signatures:
a second ballot from the same key is recognised and discarded, but the
voter stays anonymous, so trying costs nothing. The one-time traceable
ring signature of Scafuro and Zhang (ESORICS~2021) is built from a hash
function alone and enforces exactly the rule of a poll: a key that signs
once is anonymous in its ring, and a key that produces any second,
distinct signature, even of the same message, is exposed. We describe and
implement a voting design that turns this exposure into an incentive on a
permissionless proof-of-work chain. Registering a voter locks a deposit,
which the chain burns if the key is traced; voters sign inside
fixed-size groups drawn from a block-hash beacon after registration
closes; and in every round but the last a voter may spend its key's one
use on moving its right to a fresh key that is regrouped, which widens
its anonymity set. Keys carry a public label that binds them to their
single use, which replaces the proof of possession that a one-time key
cannot give. On the way we found that Fig.~1 of the scheme accepts keys
with zero rows, which sign any number of times untraced; one check fixes
it. Signatures are linear in the ring and large, $2\,064$~bytes per member
at $\lambda = 128$, so groups are a precondition: with groups of~8 a
ballot is 16.5~kB and verifies in 1.3~ms whatever the electorate, against
2~MB and 0.16~s with one ring of 1\,000. Against the Fujisaki--Suzuki
scheme of the authors' 2021--22 prototype, measured on the same machine,
verification is twice as fast and signatures 30 times larger, and a
ballot does not become readable to a future quantum computer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================================== %

Ring signatures~\cite{RST01} let a signer prove membership in a set of
public keys without revealing which one it holds, which makes them a
natural ballot primitive. A plain ring signature, however, lets a voter
sign as often as it likes. \emph{Linkable} ring
signatures~\cite{LWW04,CLSAG2020} attach a tag that is equal for two
signatures by the same key, so duplicates can be dropped.
\emph{Traceable} ring signatures~\cite{FS07} go further: two signatures by
the same key on different messages reveal the key itself, and an honest
signer cannot be framed.

Linkability answers ``is this a duplicate?''. Traceability also answers
``who did it?'', and on a chain with a native currency that answer can
carry an automatic consequence: a deposit that the protocol burns when
the trace fires. Nobody adjudicates, because the evidence is two valid
signatures that anyone can check.

Scafuro and Zhang~\cite{SZ21} define \emph{one-time} traceable ring
signatures, in which a key may sign once, ever, and build one from a
random oracle alone: keys are Naor bit-commitment keys in equivocal mode,
and two signatures by one key reveal both openings of one commitment.
Their motivating application is a blockchain where every key is used
once. A poll is the same situation: one voter, one ballot. This was the
primitive the authors meant to use in their 2021--22 design, whose
prototype instead implemented the Fujisaki--Suzuki scheme with two bugs,
one of which removed anonymity altogether (\cref{sec:legacy}).

\subsection{Research question}
\label{sec:rq}

\emph{Can a one-time traceable ring signature, an identity revealed only
on a second use, be turned into an enforceable deterrent for voting on a
permissionless chain, and what do the groups it requires cost?}
Concretely: what a deposit burned on tracing deters and what it does
not; how a one-time key can be bound to its single use without spending
it; how groups can be formed so that no participant chooses them;
whether voters can recover anonymity lost to small groups by moving
between them; and what all of this costs, in particular against the
discrete-logarithm scheme of the original prototype.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The design} (\cref{sec:design}): per-voter deposits burned
      on tracing; groups of $k$ to $2k-1$ members ordered by a hash keyed
      with the block hash that precedes the freeze; ``vote or move''
      rounds in which a key spends its one use either on a ballot or on
      a key transfer; key labels in place of a proof of possession; a
      lifecycle driven only by block timestamps.
\item \textbf{An implementation of~\cite{SZ21}} (\texttt{otrs/}) at
      $\lambda = 128$ and $256$ over SHAKE256, with deterministic signing,
      trace that returns every culprit of a coalition, and trace from
      cached openings at the cost of an XOR.
\item \textbf{A gap in Fig.~1 of~\cite{SZ21}} (\cref{sec:zerorow}): keys
      with zero rows are never traced. We give the attack, a test that
      runs it, and the one-line check that removes it.
\item \textbf{The design as consensus rules} (\texttt{chain/},
      \texttt{voting/}): a SHA-256 proof-of-work chain whose block
      validation enforces difficulty, fork choice, deposits, labels,
      groups, rounds, per-group trace bookkeeping, tally and settlement.
\item \textbf{Analysis} (\cref{sec:analysis}) of deterrence, framing,
      coalitions, beacon bias, sybils, transfer anonymity, per-group
      leakage and quantum adversaries.
\item \textbf{Evaluation} (\cref{sec:eval}): both schemes on one machine,
      cost against group size, end-to-end chain cost, and the anonymity
      left to honest voters under sybil registration, before and after
      transfers.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Non-goals}

We do not provide receipt-freeness or coercion resistance, a
peer-to-peer network layer, a side-channel-hardened implementation, or
machine-checked proofs. The chain is a reference model in which the
rules can be executed and tested, not a deployable network.

\section{Preliminaries}
% =========================================================================== %

$\lambda$ is the security parameter. $\Gf : \bits^\lambda \to
\bits^{3\lambda}$ and $\Hf : \bits^* \to \bits^\lambda$ are modelled as
random oracles. For a string $x$, $x[j]$ is its $j$-th bit.

\paragraph{Naor's bit commitment~\cite{Naor91}.} The receiver sends a
random $\rho \in \bits^{3\lambda}$; to commit to a bit $b$ the sender picks
a seed $r$ and sends $c = \Gf(r) \oplus b \cdot \rho$; to open it, it sends
$(b, r)$. The commitment is statistically binding for a random $\rho$:
opening both ways needs $\Gf(r_0) \oplus \Gf(r_1) = \rho$, and the
$2^{2\lambda}$ such sums cover a $2^{-\lambda}$ fraction of
$\bits^{3\lambda}$. In \emph{equivocal mode} the receiver's string is
built as $\rho = \Gf(s^0) \oplus \Gf(s^1)$: whoever knows $(s^0, s^1)$
commits with $c = \Gf(s^0)$ and opens it as $0$ with $s^0$ or as $1$ with
$s^1$, since $\Gf(s^1) \oplus \rho = \Gf(s^0)$. Without the seeds, such a
$\rho$ is indistinguishable from a random one.

\begin{assumption}
$\Gf$ and $\Hf$ are random oracles; we instantiate them as SHAKE256 with
distinct domain-separation prefixes that also carry $\lambda$
(\texttt{SZ21-OTRS-SHAKE256-v1/L128/G/} and so on).
\end{assumption}

No discrete logarithms, factoring or lattices are involved. The best known
quantum speed-up against a random oracle is generic (Grover); the security
proofs of~\cite{SZ21} are in the classical random-oracle model, and a
proof in the quantum random-oracle model is open.

\section{The one-time traceable ring signature}
\label{sec:construction}
% =========================================================================== %

We follow $\Sigma_{\mathrm{OTS}}$ of~\cite[Fig.~1]{SZ21}, with a context
string and a key label added (\cref{sec:ours}).

\paragraph{\KeyGen.} Pick $2\lambda$ seeds $s^0_j, s^1_j \in
\bits^\lambda$ and publish the $\lambda$ rows $\pk_j = \Gf(s^0_j) \oplus
\Gf(s^1_j)$, one equivocal Naor key per bit of the target below. A public
key is $3\lambda^2$ bits: 6\,144~bytes at $\lambda = 128$.

\paragraph{$\Sign(\sk_l, R, m, \ctx)$,} signer at position $l$ of the
ring $R = (\pk_1, \ldots, \pk_N)$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item for every $i \ne l$ pick $x_i \sample \bits^\lambda$ and commit to
      it bit by bit, $c_{i,j} = \Gf(r_{i,j}) \oplus x_i[j] \cdot \pk_{i,j}$;
\item for the signer, $c_{l,j} = \Gf(s^0_{l,j})$, committed to nothing yet;
\item $z = \Hf(\ctx, R, m, c_{1,1}, \ldots, c_{N,\lambda})$;
\item $x_l = z \oplus \bigoplus_{i \ne l} x_i$, and open the signer's
      commitments to it: $r_{l,j} = s^{x_l[j]}_{l,j}$;
\item output all $x_i$ and $r_{i,j}$: $N\lambda(\lambda + 1)$ bits.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{\Verify} recomputes every $c_{i,j}$ from $(x_i[j], r_{i,j})$,
recomputes $z$ and accepts iff $\bigoplus_i x_i = z$. It costs $N\lambda$
evaluations of $\Gf$ and one of $\Hf$.

\paragraph{\Trace.} Two valid signatures with openings $\alpha_{i,j}$ and
$\beta_{i,j}$ over the same ring are traced to member $i$ if
$\Gf(\alpha_{i,j}) \oplus \Gf(\beta_{i,j}) = \pk_{i,j}$ for some row $j$:
together they reveal both seeds of one of $i$'s rows. Two signatures by an
honest key have different targets, so with probability $1 - 2^{-\lambda}$
their $x_l$ differ in some bit $j$, and the key has revealed $s^0_{l,j}$
in one and $s^1_{l,j}$ in the other. This holds \emph{even on the same
message}: the commitments of the other members are fresh, so the target
is new. A copy of a signature is byte-identical and traces nobody.

\paragraph{Security~\cite{SZ21}.} In the random-oracle model: one-time
\emph{anonymity} (a key that signs once is hidden among the ring);
\emph{public traceability} ($N$ keys cannot produce $N + 1$ valid
signatures on one ring without two of them tracing a key; in particular
a key cannot sign twice untraced); and one-time \emph{exculpability} (an
honest key that signs once cannot be traced, whatever the adversary
publishes). All three need every honest key to sign at most once.

\subsection{What we add}
\label{sec:ours}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Non-zero rows} (\cref{sec:zerorow}).
\item \textbf{A context string} hashed into $z$, here the poll and round
      (\texttt{e-ring-voting/v1/}$\,\|\,$\textit{election\_id}$\,\|\,$\ldots),
      and a $2\lambda$-bit digest of the ring in place of the ring itself.
\item \textbf{A 32-byte public label inside every key}, hashed into the
      ring with the rows. The application decides what it means; here it
      names the single use the key was made for (\cref{sec:labels}).
\item \textbf{Deterministic signing.} The $x_i$ and $r_{i,j}$ are a
      SHAKE256 stream keyed with the master secret, the label, the
      context, the ring digest and the message. For ordinary signatures
      this is hygiene; here it is necessary, because a one-time key that
      re-signs the same ballot with fresh randomness is traced. A wallet
      that retries a broadcast produces a copy, not a second signature.
\item \textbf{Trace returns every culprit.} A signature produced by a
      coalition can open rows of several of its keys.
\item \textbf{Trace from openings.} Verification computes $\Gf(r_{i,j})$
      for every seed anyway. We keep them, as one big integer per
      signature, together with the concatenation of the ring's rows; two
      signatures then trace iff $A \oplus B \oplus P$ has an all-zero
      $3\lambda$-bit row, which is a single XOR (0.04~ms for $N = 8$
      instead of 2~ms).
\end{enumerate}
The secret key is a $2\lambda$-bit master secret from which the label and
SHAKE256 derive the $2\lambda$ seeds, so a key file holds 32~bytes at
$\lambda = 128$.

\subsection{Keys with zero rows}
\label{sec:zerorow}

\begin{proposition}
Let $\pk^\ast$ be a key whose rows are all zero. A member holding no
secret for $\pk^\ast$ beyond its position can sign at that position any
number of times, and no two of its signatures are traced to $\pk^\ast$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Argument]
With $\pk^\ast_j = 0$ the commitment $\Gf(r) \oplus b \cdot \pk^\ast_j$
equals $\Gf(r)$ for both bits. The signer commits $c_j = \Gf(r_j)$ for
fresh seeds $r_j$, computes $z$ and $x^\ast$ as in \Sign, and opens every
row with $r_j$ whatever $x^\ast[j]$ is; the signature verifies. Two such
signatures open row $j$ with independent seeds $r_j, r'_j$, and the trace
condition $\Gf(r_j) \oplus \Gf(r'_j) = 0$ fails.
\end{proof}

The verification of~\cite[Fig.~1]{SZ21} checks that the ring's keys are
distinct and well formed, not that rows are non-zero, so a voter that
registers such a key (it is a legitimate output of \KeyGen{} with
$s^0_j = s^1_j$) votes as often as it likes and never loses its deposit.
The traceability proof implicitly assumes that every row is non-zero.
Our \texttt{Ring} refuses any key with a zero row;
\texttt{tests/test\_otrs.py::TestZeroRow} runs the attack with the check
disabled and shows it refused otherwise.

A key with \emph{one} zero row is also refused. It would let its owner
open that row both ways, but every other row still traces, so it gives
nothing beyond the check. For a non-zero row, opening it both ways twice
without a trace would require two seed pairs with the same XOR, a 4-XOR
problem on $3\lambda$ bits that costs about $2^\lambda$ with Wagner's
algorithm~\cite{Wagner02}; this is what the $3\lambda$ stretch of $\Gf$
buys.

\section{Design}
\label{sec:design}
% =========================================================================== %

\subsection{Participants and assets}

The chain has an account ledger of \emph{eVotes} keyed by Ed25519 public
keys. A \emph{creator} opens a poll; a \emph{sponsor} registers voters and
pays for them (it may be the voter, the creator or anyone else);
\emph{voters} hold one-time keys; \emph{miners} produce blocks. Ballots
and key transfers are the only transactions without an account: no
sender, no chain-layer signature and no fee, so they do not link a voter
to an account.

\subsection{Poll parameters}

A \textsf{setup\_poll} transaction, signed by the creator, fixes the
options and
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item the schedule $\mathit{registration\_close} \le \mathit{voting\_open}
      < \mathit{voting\_close}$, with $\mathit{voting\_open}$ in the future;
\item the \emph{group size} $k$ ($0$ for one ring, otherwise $k \ge 2$);
\item the number of \emph{rounds} $r \ge 1$, which split the voting
      window evenly;
\item the \emph{deposit} $D \ge 0$ locked per registered voter;
\item the security parameter $\lambda \in \{128, 256\}$ of the poll's keys.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Registration and key labels}
\label{sec:labels}

A \textsf{register\_voter} transaction is signed by the sponsor and
carries the voter's public key and a handle. It is valid only before
$\mathit{registration\_close}$, for a well-formed key at the poll's
$\lambda$, not yet in the poll, and whose label is
\[
\mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}(\texttt{e-ring-voting/register-v1} \,\|\,
\mathit{election\_id} \,\|\, |\mathit{handle}| \,\|\, \mathit{handle}).
\]
The sponsor pays the fee (to the miner) and $D$ (to the poll's escrow,
which no account controls).

The 2022 procedure had the voter sign its own registration. A one-time
key cannot do that without spending its only signature. The label gives
what that signature was for: the key was made for this poll and this
handle, so a registration seen in the mempool cannot be replayed under
another handle or into another poll, and the same key cannot be
registered twice. Labels do not prove possession; a sponsor can register
a key nobody holds, which is a sybil that abstains (\cref{sec:sybils}).

\subsection{Groups drawn from a beacon}
\label{sec:groups}

Phase changes happen at the start of the first block whose timestamp
crosses a deadline. The first block with timestamp $\ge
\mathit{voting\_open}$ freezes the registered keys $P$, identified by the
SHA-256 fingerprints of their encodings, and splits them into groups using
as beacon $\beta$ that block's $\mathit{prev\_hash}$:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item if $k = 0$ or $|P| < 2k$, one group $P$;
\item otherwise sort $P$ by $\mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}(\dst_G \,\|\, I_0
      \,\|\, \beta \,\|\, \mathit{fp})$, cut it into $g = \lfloor |P|/k
      \rfloor$ consecutive groups and give the $|P| \bmod g$ leftovers
      one each, so every group has between $k$ and $2k-1$ members;
\item each group is sorted by fingerprint, which is its ring order.
\end{enumerate}
A poll with fewer than two registrations is aborted and its deposits
refunded. Every key was registered and the creator's choices were final
before $\beta$ existed, so neither can place a key next to a target. The
miner of the block that becomes $\beta$ has some influence
(\cref{sec:bias}).

\subsection{Rounds: vote or move}
\label{sec:transfers}

In round $\rho$ a key in group $g$ spends its one use by signing, on the
ring $g$ with context $\ctx_\rho = \texttt{e-ring-voting/v1/}\,\|\,I_\rho$,
either
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item a \textbf{ballot}, message $\texttt{ballot-v1} \,\|\, \mathit{choice}$, or
\item a \textbf{key transfer}, message $\texttt{key-transfer-v1} \,\|\,
      \pk'$, naming a fresh key $\pk'$ whose label is
      $\mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}(\texttt{e-ring-voting/transfer-v1} \,\|\,
      \mathit{election\_id} \,\|\, \rho + 1)$ (allowed in every round but
      the last; $\pk'$ must not already belong to the poll).
\end{itemize}
A ballot and a transfer by one key are two signatures and are traced. At
the first block past the end of round $\rho$, the accepted transfers'
target keys are grouped again with the same algorithm, the issue
$I_{\rho+1}$ and a fresh beacon. Messages are accepted only in their own
round, and only if they verify against the group they name.

This is the transfer ``to another group with another key'' of the 2022
design, and also how~\cite[\S1.1]{SZ21} propose to use one-time keys on a
chain: each use also certifies the next key. The observer sees which
group a transfer was signed in, not by whom, so a ballot cast by $\pk'$ in
round $\rho + 1$ hides among all round-$\rho$ members of the origin groups
of every key in its new group (\cref{sec:anon-transfer}).

\subsection{Admission and classification}
\label{sec:admission}

Each group keeps a \emph{tag book} (\texttt{voting/tally.py}) holding the
openings of the signatures recorded so far. A ballot or transfer that
verifies is traced against all of them and classified:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{replay}: byte-identical to a recorded one, invalid;
\item \textbf{fresh}: traces nobody, recorded;
\item \textbf{evidence}: traces at least one key not yet exposed,
      recorded, and the key is now a culprit;
\item \textbf{saturated}: traces only keys already exposed, invalid
      (there is nothing left to learn);
\item \textbf{exhausted}: would be the $(N+1)$-th untraced signature on a
      ring of $N$, which public traceability rules out; invalid, as a
      guard.
\end{itemize}
A key therefore contributes at most two messages to the chain, and a copy
of someone else's ballot cannot be included at all. At the close, every
recorded message that traces a culprit, or is traced by one, is void; the
others are counted.

\subsection{Close and settlement}

At the first block with timestamp $\ge \mathit{voting\_close}$ the chain
computes the tally from the accepted ballots of every round, publishes
the culprits per round with the handle each was registered under,
\emph{burns} the deposit of every round-0 culprit, and refunds every other
deposit to its sponsor. Burned eVotes go to nobody: a miner that received
them would profit from evidence it could manufacture with keys it had
registered itself. No transaction publishes the result; every node
computes it while replaying the chain (\texttt{chain/auditor.py}).

Keys that arrived by transfer have no deposit behind them, since tying
them to one would undo the transfer's unlinkability. A second use of
such a key voids its messages but burns nothing.

\subsection{Chain}

Blocks carry $(\mathit{prev\_hash}, \mathit{height}, \mathit{difficulty},
\mathit{timestamp}, \mathit{miner}, \mathit{tx\_root}, \mathit{nonce})$
and a transaction list; the proof of work is $\mathrm{SHA\text{-}256}$ of
the header having $\mathit{difficulty}$ leading zero bits. As in the
prototype, the difficulty moves by one bit when the median interval of the
last 11 blocks leaves $[T/2, 3T/2]$ around $T = 60$\,s; unlike the
prototype, the required difficulty is a validation rule. Nodes follow the
valid chain with the largest $\sum 2^{\mathit{difficulty}}$. Block
application is atomic, and the coinbase must equal reward plus fees.

% =========================================================================== %
\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}
% =========================================================================== %

The threat model is in \texttt{paper/threat\_model.md}. We assume random
oracles for SHAKE256, that honest keys sign once, Ed25519 unforgeability
during the poll, and an honest majority of hash power.

\subsection{What the deposit deters, and what it does not}

\textbf{Double-voting does not pay.} Any second signature of a key in a
round, a second ballot, the same ballot re-signed, or a ballot and a
transfer, traces it and voids every message involved. In particular the
attack that transfers make possible, voting in round $\rho$ and moving to
a key that votes again in round $\rho + 1$, voids the ballot and drops the
transfer before regrouping.

\textbf{It costs.} The key is named on chain with its handle and, for a
registered key, its sponsor loses $D$. This gives the sponsor, often the
organisation that vouched for the voter, a reason to vet voters, and a
voter a reason not to hand a copy of its key to someone else: if both use
it, both lose.

\textbf{Coalitions.} $t$ colluding keys in a group can pool their seeds
and split the rows they open among their keys, but public traceability
bounds them to $t$ untraced signatures. They choose \emph{which} of their
keys is exposed, not whether one is.

\textbf{It cannot frame.} By one-time exculpability, an honest key that
signs once is never traced. A replay is byte-identical and refused;
copying an honest signer's revealed seeds into a new signature reveals
nothing new, since the trace needs the \emph{other} seed of a row, which
only the signer knows.

\textbf{What it does not do.} The deposit prices misbehaviour, not
registration: a sybil key that behaves gets its deposit back. Fees and
the sponsor's on-chain identity price sybils. Nor does the deposit stop a
voter who sells its vote and abstains.

\subsection{Who chooses the groups}
\label{sec:bias}

Registrations close before $\beta$ exists. The miner of the block that
becomes $\beta$ can discard a block whose hash gives an assignment it
dislikes and mine another, and can choose its timestamp to decide which
block crosses $\mathit{voting\_open}$. Each discarded block costs its
reward and the work to replace it. If one draw isolates a given honest
voter (no other honest member in its group) with probability $p$, the
adversary needs about $1/p$ discarded blocks. With half of the keys
adversarial, \cref{tab:anon} gives $p \approx 0.8\%$ for $k = 8$, about
130 discarded blocks per target, and $p \approx 13\%$ for $k = 4$, no
grinding needed. A VDF or commit--reveal beacon would remove the bias.

\subsection{Sybils}
\label{sec:sybils}

With sybil share $s$, an honest voter in a group of $k$ expects about
$1 + (k-1)(1-s)$ honest members. Sybils cannot be detected, since a sybil
used once behaves like an honest voter; this is the problem every
group-based anonymity system faces~\cite{Buddies2013}. The design can only
make sybils costly and pick $k$ against the expected sybil share.

\subsection{Anonymity after transfers}
\label{sec:anon-transfer}

Let $O(\pk')$ be the round-$\rho$ group in which the transfer to $\pk'$
was signed. A ballot by $\pk'$ in round-$(\rho+1)$ group $g'$ could have
been cast by any round-$\rho$ member of $\bigcup_{\pk'' \in g'}
O(\pk'')$, which contains the voter's own group. Under this observer
model a transfer never shrinks the anonymity set and widens it by up to
$|g'|$ origin groups. An observer that counts how many members of each
origin group voted or moved can narrow it further; the metric is an upper
bound for such an observer.

\subsection{What groups leak}

Ballots name their group, so per-group tallies are public: a group that
votes unanimously reveals every member's vote. This is a second reason,
besides sybils, not to make $k$ small; transfers help, because next-round
groups mix voters from different origins.

\subsection{Quantum adversaries}

A ballot hides its signer because the seeds it reveals at the signer's
position look like the fresh seeds at every other position. Telling them
apart without the secret means inverting $\Gf$ on $\lambda$-bit seeds,
$2^\lambda$ classically and about $2^{\lambda/2}$ with Grover;
$\lambda = 256$ keeps a 128-bit margin. A ballot under~\cite{FS07}
instead carries $h^{x}$ for the signer's discrete logarithm $x$; once
discrete logarithms are cheap, every recorded ballot names its signer. A
public chain is a permanent archive of ballots, and ballot secrecy has to
outlive the poll by decades, while the validity of signatures only matters
until the close. The accounts (Ed25519) protect only the latter, so a
classical signature suffices there. The proofs of~\cite{SZ21} are in the
classical random-oracle model; a QROM proof is open.

\subsection{Censorship and front-running}

Anonymous transactions pay no fee, so a miner has no direct incentive to
include them; with an honest majority they are eventually included. A
transfer cannot be redirected, because the target key is inside the
signed message, and a front-run copy only includes the same transfer
earlier. A message submitted after its round is invalid, so a censoring
majority can disenfranchise; we do not address this.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Transferred keys cannot be slashed}: a trade-off between
      accountability and the unlinkability the transfer exists for.
\item \textbf{Size.} Signatures are $\Theta(N\lambda^2)$ bits; groups are
      not an optimisation but a precondition.
\item \textbf{Beacon bias} (\cref{sec:bias}).
\item \textbf{No coercion resistance}: a voter can prove its ballot by
      revealing its master secret.
\item \textbf{Chain security is economic}~\cite{ParkSpecter2021}.
\item \textbf{Implementation}: pure Python, not constant time; the chain
      has no network layer.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:impl}
% =========================================================================== %

\begin{lstlisting}[style=shell]

voting/   records.py groups.py tally.py     # poll records, groups, tag books
chain/    block.py transactions.py state.py mining.py node.py
          client.py auditor.py cli.py       # the design as consensus rules
fs07/     group.py hash.py scheme.py serialize.py _libsodium.py   # comparison
tests/    164 tests     bench/  bench_primitives bench_groups bench_chain
\end{lstlisting}

\paragraph{Primitive.} \texttt{otrs} depends only on \texttt{hashlib}.
$\Gf$ copies a pre-absorbed SHAKE256 state per call. A key encodes as
$\mathit{ident} \,\|\, \mathit{label} \,\|\, \mathit{rows}$ (6\,177~bytes
at $\lambda = 128$, 24\,609 at 256), a signature as $\mathit{ident}
\,\|\, N \,\|\, (x_i, r_{i,1..\lambda})_i$; decoding rejects every
malformed length and every zero row. \texttt{Ring.check} returns the
openings that the tag book keeps for trace.

\paragraph{Consensus.} \texttt{chain/state.py} holds every rule of
\cref{sec:design}: phase changes at block timestamps (also used by clients
to learn the groups the next block will use), escrow and settlement,
labels, and the per-group tag books. \texttt{chain/client.py} makes
labelled keys and builds registrations, ballots and transfers. The CLI
mines one block per command with an explicit timestamp, so a complete poll
with groups, a transfer and a double vote runs in two seconds (README).

\paragraph{Tests.} 164 tests (\texttt{pytest}) cover the primitive (every
position, both parameter sets, encodings, rejection, trace on the same
and different messages, coalitions, exculpability, the zero-row attack),
classification, group assignment, and the chain (difficulty, fork choice,
ledger, labels, $\lambda$ mismatch, groups, rounds, transfers,
vote-and-move, double votes with fresh randomness, settlement).
The FS07 comparison has its own tests, including RFC~9380 vectors.

Two elements are new: the forfeit is a deposit per voter, triggered by the
trace (in 2022 it was locked by the poll's manager), and labels replace
the voter-signed registration, which a one-time key cannot provide.

Reading the 2021--22 prototype back turned up two bugs in its FS07 code,
each verified by running it:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{The hash into the group had a known discrete logarithm.} It
      mapped data to $g^{t}$ with $t$ derived from the data
      (\texttt{ring.py:111--120}). Anyone could compute $t$ for $h$ and
      then $\pk_j^{\,t} = h^{x_j}$ for every member, and compare with a
      signature: the signer's position followed from public data alone.
      Signatures verified and traced correctly, so no demo showed it.
\item \textbf{$1/i$ was computed modulo $p$} instead of the group order
      $q$ (\texttt{ring.py:147}). Only the signer at position~1 produced
      verifying signatures; the demo signed at position~1.
\end{itemize}
The node also adjusted difficulty in opposite directions when mining and
when validating, and neither layer was connected to votes. The fixed FS07
(Ristretto255, RFC~9380 hash-to-curve) is kept in \texttt{fs07/} as the
baseline of \cref{tab:bench,tab:groupcost}.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
% =========================================================================== %

\textbf{Traceable and one-time schemes.} Fujisaki and Suzuki~\cite{FS07}
introduced traceable ring signatures and listed voting among their
applications; Fujisaki~\cite{Fujisaki2011} gives a sub-linear variant
without random oracles. Scafuro and Zhang~\cite{SZ21} define the
one-time variant and build it from hash functions; Branco and
Mateus~\cite{BrancoMateus2018} give a code-based post-quantum traceable
ring signature whose signatures are much larger. Linkable schemes reach
logarithmic size (Triptych~\cite{Triptych2020}, one-out-of-many
proofs~\cite{GK15}), and $k$-linkable signatures~\cite{KLinkable2025}
relax ``one use'' in another direction.

\textbf{Ring signatures for voting.} Chirotonia~\cite{Chirotonia2021}
runs elections on Ethereum with linkable ring signatures: one ring,
duplicates discarded, no penalty. This is the common pattern.

\textbf{Slashable anonymity.} A proposal for privacy-preserving Casper
FFG~\cite{CasperTRS} uses traceable ring signatures so that validators
stay anonymous unless they equivocate, in which case they lose their
deposit. Our deposits apply the principle to voters; groups, beacon
assignment, transfers and labels have no counterpart there.

\textbf{Groups and intersection attacks.} The effect of sybils and of
repeated observations on anonymity groups is studied in
Buddies~\cite{Buddies2013}. Our transfers are the reverse operation: a
voter leaves its group on purpose, once, to enlarge its set.

\textbf{Blockchain voting.} Park et al.~\cite{ParkSpecter2021} argue that
blockchains add attack surface to Internet voting; the design does not
answer their objections about client security and coercion.

To our knowledge, no published system combines a one-time traceable ring
signature with a deposit burned on tracing, beacon-assigned voter groups
and anonymity-widening key transfers.

\section{Open problems}
\label{sec:future}
% =========================================================================== %

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Slashing transferred keys} without linking them to their
      origin.
\item \textbf{An unbiasable beacon}: a VDF over $\mathit{prev\_hash}$ or a
      commit--reveal among sponsors.
\item \textbf{Pricing}: deposit and registration fee for a given $k$ and
      adversary budget; incentives to include fee-less ballots.
\item \textbf{Per-group tally leakage}: tallying across groups without
      publishing per-group counts.
\item \textbf{Shorter one-time traceable signatures} from hash functions,
      sub-linear in the ring, which would allow larger groups.
\item \textbf{Security of~\cite{SZ21} in the quantum random-oracle
      model}, and formal models of the protocol (ProVerif, Tamarin).
\item \textbf{Coercion resistance}~\cite{JCJ2005,Civitas2008}.
\end{enumerate}
