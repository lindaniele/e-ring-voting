\section{What's the point?}
% ===========================================================================

We built a small system for running \textbf{anonymous online votes}
that also lets anyone in the world \textbf{check the result}. Think of
a class president election, a board vote in a company, or a small
referendum --- not a national election.

Two properties have to be true at the same time:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Nobody can tell how you voted.} Not the people running
        the election, not the developers, not anyone who reads the
        public record of the vote.
  \item \textbf{Anyone can recompute the result.} Given the public
        record of the election, you can do the count yourself, with
        your own laptop, and refuse to accept any other claimed result.
\end{itemize}

If you stop and think about it for a minute, those two properties look
like they contradict each other. ``The votes are secret'' usually
means ``trust us, you can't see them.'' ``Anyone can recompute''
usually means ``the votes are public.'' How can both be true?

The whole project is built around one cryptographic trick that breaks
the contradiction, plus one idea of ours on top: the same trick can
make cheating \emph{cost money}. The rest of these notes explain the
trick (in words), our idea, what we built, and where the limits are.

% ===========================================================================
\section{The contradiction, in concrete terms}
% ===========================================================================

Imagine your class is voting on lunch. The options are pasta, pizza,
\textit{focaccia}. You write your choice on a slip of paper and drop
it in a sealed box. At the end of the period, the teacher opens the
box and counts.

What can go wrong?

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{The teacher cheats on the count.} You can't tell, because
        you're not the one counting. Maybe the teacher swaps two
        ballots, or pretends one didn't exist.
  \item \textbf{The teacher peeks at who voted for what.} You can't tell,
        because they own the box.
  \item \textbf{Someone votes twice.} The teacher might or might not
        notice; you certainly can't.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{The naive online version is worse.} The website stores your
vote in a database. The database administrator can read it. The server
can lie about the count. You have to trust a stranger to be honest, and
even if they are honest, you have to trust they wrote the code right.

What we want is a system where:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Your ballot, the very moment you cast it, is mixed in with
        everyone else's so well that even the people running the
        election can't tell which one is yours.
  \item Every ballot is written down in a public place that anyone
        can read.
  \item The rules of the system make it \emph{impossible} for a
        cheater to publish a fake count without it being publicly
        obvious.
\end{enumerate}

Property~1 sounds the most magical. Property~2 sounds easy. Property~3
sounds nice but vague. The next sections build up each one.

\section{The magic envelope: ring signatures}
% ===========================================================================

Here is the key idea, in an analogy.

\begin{aside}[The crowd analogy]
Suppose 8 people are in a room behind a curtain. A note slides out under
the curtain, signed \emph{``one of the 8 people in this room.''} You can
check that it really came from one of them, but there is no way to tell
\emph{which}.

A \textbf{ring signature} is the digital version of that. It proves
\emph{``one of these 8 people signed this''} without revealing which one.
The list of people is called the \emph{ring}.
\end{aside}

So if every voter has a secret key and publishes a public key, a ballot
can be built like this:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item Alice wants to vote for pasta.
  \item She makes a ring signature on the word ``pasta'', with a ring of
        8 public keys, including hers.
  \item Anyone can check it: \emph{one of these 8 registered voters}
        voted for pasta.
  \item Nobody can tell it was Alice. Not even the organiser.
\end{itemize}

That is property 1 from before. The ballot is public, the voter is hidden
in the crowd.

% ===========================================================================
\section{The double-vote problem}
% ===========================================================================

If the ballot hides the voter, what stops Alice from voting twice? A plain
ring signature does not. The kind we use, a \textbf{one-time traceable ring
signature} (invented by Alessandra Scafuro and Bihan Zhang in 2021), has a
simple rule: \emph{each key may sign once}. Sign once and you are invisible.
Sign a second time, \emph{anything at all}, and everybody learns which key
it was.

Here is how it works, with boxes instead of maths.

\begin{aside}[Trick boxes]
Every voter owns 128 little \textbf{trick boxes}, lined up in a row, and
shows the row to everyone: that row is the public key.

\medskip
A normal box can be closed with a ``0'' or a ``1'' inside, and later
opened to show which. Anybody can close anybody's box, but once it is
closed it can only be opened to show what was really put in.

\medskip
A trick box has a secret: its owner holds \emph{two} little keys for it, a
``0-key'' and a ``1-key''. With those, the owner can close the box
\emph{empty} and decide \emph{later} whether it opens as 0 or as 1. Nobody
else can do that.

\medskip
And here is the catch, the heart of the whole project: \textbf{if anyone
ever sees both keys of the same box, they can check that both fit, and
they know whose box it is.}
\end{aside}

\textbf{Signing a ballot.} Alice closes 128 boxes for each of the 8 people
in the ring, using the other seven people's boxes honestly (with random 0s
and 1s that she picks) and her own boxes empty. Then a \emph{lottery
machine} (a hash function) looks at everything she closed and at the word
``pasta'' and prints a random 128-digit number of 0s and 1s. The rule for a
valid signature: the 8 rows of 0s and 1s inside the boxes must ``add up''
to the lottery number. Alice could not know the number in advance, so the
seven honest rows will not add up by themselves; but her own boxes are
still undecided, so she opens each one as whatever digit makes the sum
right. She shows a 0-key here, a 1-key there.

\textbf{Checking.} Anyone opens all the boxes, runs the lottery machine
again, and checks the sum. Every box opens correctly. Nothing shows which
row was the flexible one, so Alice is hidden among the 8.

\textbf{Signing twice.} If Alice signs again, even ``pasta'' again, the
lottery prints a new number. Her own boxes have to open differently in at
least one place: a box that showed its 0-key last time must now show its
1-key. Now both keys of that box are public, and anyone can see that they
fit Alice's box. She is named.

This is the cleverest part of the whole system. The same signature
\emph{protects} you when you behave and \emph{exposes} you the moment you
cheat, and nobody has to judge you: the two signatures are the proof.

\begin{warning}
``Once'' really means once. Signing the same ballot twice also gets you
caught. So our program never makes up fresh random numbers when it signs:
it computes them from your secret and the ballot, so if your computer
resends a ballot, it sends an exact copy, which is harmless.
\end{warning}

\begin{aside}[We found a hole]
What if somebody makes a ``box'' that is not really a box: one where the
0-key and the 1-key are the same key? Then any key opens it either way, and
showing it twice reveals nothing. A cheater with a row of such fake boxes
could vote as often as they liked and never be caught. The original paper
does not check for this. Our program does: a public key with a fake box is
refused.
\end{aside}

\section{Where do you write everything down?}
% ===========================================================================

The ballots have to live somewhere public, where anyone can read them and
nobody can erase them. We use a \textbf{blockchain}, the same kind of
public record Bitcoin uses: a list of \emph{blocks}, each containing some
records and a pointer to the block before it.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
  every node/.style={font=\small},
  entry/.style={draw, rounded corners=2pt, minimum width=2.6cm, minimum height=1.2cm, fill=blue!4},
]
\node[entry] (e0) {\shortstack{Block 0\\\itshape start}};
\node[entry, right=0.4cm of e0] (e1) {\shortstack{Block 1\\\itshape poll opens}};
\node[entry, right=0.4cm of e1] (e2) {\shortstack{Block 2\\\itshape Alice registers}};
\node[entry, right=0.4cm of e2] (e3) {\shortstack{Block 3\\\itshape Bob registers}};
\node[entry, right=0.4cm of e3] (e4) {\shortstack{Block 4\\\itshape a ballot}};
\draw[->, gray] (e1) -- (e0);
\draw[->, gray] (e2) -- (e1);
\draw[->, gray] (e3) -- (e2);
\draw[->, gray] (e4) -- (e3);
\node[below=0.2cm of e2, gray] {\textit{each block points back to the one before it}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

If anyone silently edits an old block, the pointers break. Who adds the
next block? Anyone who wins a small puzzle: their computer guesses random
numbers until one of them, when hashed, starts with enough zeros. That is
called \emph{proof of work}. Nobody is in charge, and every computer that
follows the chain checks every rule itself.

\begin{aside}[Why the puzzle?]
It makes taking over the record expensive. To rewrite history you would
have to redo all that guessing faster than everyone else put together.
The flip side, pointed out by Park, Specter, Narula and Rivest (MIT, 2021):
for an election that matters a lot, someone could \emph{rent} enough
computers for the few days of the vote. A blockchain is not magic.
\end{aside}

The poll goes through phases, and the chain moves it along by the clock,
without anyone pressing a button: \textbf{registration} (voters are
added), \textbf{voting} (in one or more rounds), and \textbf{close} (at
which every computer counts the ballots by itself; nobody announces a
result that could differ from it).

\section{Our idea: make cheating cost money}
% ===========================================================================

Most systems that hide voters stop at ``if you vote twice, your extra vote
is thrown away''. That is fair, but it costs the cheater nothing, so trying
is free. Our signatures tell everybody \emph{who} cheated, and a blockchain
has money (we call it \emph{eVotes}). So:

\begin{aside}[The deposit]
When you register to vote, someone (you, or the organisation that vouches
for you) locks a small deposit, like on a rented flat. Vote once, and it
comes back after the poll. Use your key twice, and the chain names your key
and \emph{destroys} the deposit. Nobody decides this; it is a rule that
every computer checking the chain applies.
\end{aside}

Destroyed, not given to anyone: if the person who builds the next block got
the money, they would have a reason to create cheaters on purpose.

\paragraph{Keys made for one poll.} Each key carries a little public label
saying which poll and which voter name it was made for. The chain refuses
it anywhere else, so nobody can copy your registration under another name
or use your key in another poll.

\paragraph{Small groups instead of one huge crowd.} These signatures are
big: about 2 kilobytes for every person in the crowd. Hiding among 1{,}000
people would make every ballot 2 megabytes. So voters are split into groups
of, say, 8 (a ballot is then 16 kilobytes). But who decides the groups? If
the organiser did, they could put you with 7 people they control, and then
your ballot is obviously yours. So nobody decides: the groups are shuffled
using the fingerprint of a block that did not exist yet when registration
closed.

\paragraph{Changing group.} A small group is a small crowd. So in each
round, instead of voting, you may use your one signature to hand your vote
to a brand-new key, which is put into a new group in the next round.
Everybody sees that \emph{someone} in your old group moved, not who. When
you then vote from the new group, you hide among all the old groups your
new group-mates came from. Voting \emph{and} moving in the same round uses
your key twice, and you get caught.

\paragraph{Where the idea came from.} We sketched all of this in 2021--22:
groups, ``vote or move'', a forfeit separate from fees, and a one-time
signature, because a poll is exactly ``one person, one signature''. This
version finishes it.

\section{Why this kind of signature}
% ===========================================================================

Our 2021 prototype actually used an older kind of traceable signature
(Fujisaki and Suzuki, 2007), built on the same kind of maths as the
padlock in your browser. It makes much smaller ballots, about 30 times
smaller. So why switch?

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{A blockchain never forgets.} Every ballot stays on it
        forever.
  \item \textbf{A big enough quantum computer} (which does not exist yet)
        would break the padlock maths. With the old signature, it could go
        back and unmask \emph{every ballot ever cast}.
  \item The one-time signature is built from nothing but a hash function
        (the lottery machine). No known quantum trick unmasks it.
\end{itemize}

Ballots only need to be \emph{valid} until the poll closes, but they need
to stay \emph{secret} for decades. So we pay with size and get secrecy
that should last. And it is even faster to check.

\section{What this project actually built}
% ===========================================================================

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \texttt{otrs/}: the signature (trick boxes and lottery). About 750
        lines of Python, using nothing but a hash function.
  \item \texttt{voting/} and \texttt{chain/}: the rules of the poll and the
        blockchain that enforces them: deposits, labels, groups, changing
        group, counting.
  \item \texttt{fs07/}: the old kind of signature, fixed, only to compare.
\end{itemize}

Plus 164 automated tests that check the whole thing works the way we say,
including tests that \emph{attack} it: double votes, copied ballots,
groups of cheaters working together, and the fake-box trick.

Some numbers. With 1{,}000 voters in groups of 8, all ballots together take
16.5 megabytes and about one second to check on a normal computer; in one
big crowd, two gigabytes and two and a half minutes. If an attacker
registers half of all voters as fake people, a voter in a group of 8 still
hides among about 4.5 real people on average, and if half the voters change
group once, that grows to about 30.

\paragraph{Mistakes we found.} Our 2021 prototype had a bug that made the
envelope see-through: anyone could work out who signed. Nothing in the
demo showed it, because the ballots still checked out as valid. And the
paper the new signature comes from has the fake-box hole described above.
Both are fixed and tested.

\section{What this project did \emph{not} fix}
% ===========================================================================

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Coercion.} If someone threatens you (``vote pasta or
        else''), you could prove you obeyed by showing them your secret
        key. Fixing this is called \emph{coercion resistance}, and it needs
        much more machinery.
  \item \textbf{Compromised computers.} If a virus on your laptop signs
        whatever it wants, no cryptography can tell.
  \item \textbf{Small groups leak.} The result is visible per group: if all
        8 people in a group voted pasta, everyone knows how each of them
        voted. Fake voters also shrink the real crowd you hide in.
  \item \textbf{The group shuffle is not perfectly fair.} Whoever builds
        the block whose fingerprint shuffles the groups could throw it away
        and try again, at the cost of a block reward each time.
  \item \textbf{Keys you moved to cannot be fined.} A new key has no
        deposit behind it (attaching one would reveal where it came from),
        so if it signs twice its votes are thrown away but nobody pays.
  \item \textbf{Big ballots.} 16 kilobytes for a group of 8 is fine; much
        bigger groups get expensive.
  \item \textbf{Proofs.} The paper proves its signature secure against
        ordinary computers; a proof against quantum computers is still an
        open question, and our code has not been independently audited.
\end{itemize}

\section{How this connects to things you may have heard of}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Bitcoin} invented the proof-of-work chain we use. Bitcoin stores
money transfers; we store poll records, and use the money for deposits.

\paragraph{Post-quantum cryptography} is cryptography designed to survive
quantum computers. Our signature belongs to its simplest family: schemes
built only from hash functions.

\paragraph{Voting research.} Helios (2008) and ElectionGuard (2021) are
well-known verifiable voting systems. Ours sits in the same family but uses
a different building block and is much smaller in scope.

\section{Glossary}
% ===========================================================================

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.2em, style=nextline, font=\bfseries]
  \item[Anonymity set] The crowd you are hiding inside: your group, or after
        changing group, all the groups your new group-mates came from.
  \item[Ballot] A single voter's signed choice.
  \item[Blockchain] A public list of blocks, each pointing to the one
        before, that anyone can check and nobody can quietly edit.
  \item[Deposit] eVotes locked when you register, returned after the poll
        unless your key signed twice.
  \item[Group] The small crowd you sign in, shuffled by a block fingerprint
        nobody could predict at registration time.
  \item[Hash (``lottery machine'')] A short fingerprint of data. Change one
        bit of the input and the output changes completely.
  \item[Key label] A tag inside a key that says which poll and which voter
        name it was made for.
  \item[One-time] Each key signs once, ever.
  \item[Proof of work] The guessing puzzle that decides who adds the next
        block.
  \item[Ring signature] A signature that proves \emph{one of these people}
        signed it, without revealing which one.
  \item[Trick box] Our picture for one row of a public key: its owner can
        open it either way, and showing both ways reveals the owner.
\end{description}
